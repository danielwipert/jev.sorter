Jev vs. the Frontier

Same accuracy on automated decisions. One‑fiftieth of the cost.

DW Daniel WipertFounder, Chorus AI Systems

We put a small, specialized decision model, Jev, and two general-purpose models, Claude Sonnet 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5, into the same automated triage pipeline. On the customer messages each model was confident enough to handle on its own, Jev was as accurate as Sonnet 5, at about 2% of the cost.

01 · What we did

One pipeline. Three models. Rules fixed in advance.

Most business AI isn't writing essays. It's sorting: which team should get this message, is this refund request valid, what kind of ticket is this. We tested that kind of work.

01

Same job for every model

Each model read a real bank customer message and picked one of 77 categories, such as "card hasn't arrived" or "lost or stolen card", along with how confident it was.

02

Automate the sure ones

Simple checks ran first. Then only confident answers were accepted automatically, and the rest went to a person. We measured how often each model was right on what it accepted.

03

No moving goalposts

The 1,000 test messages were locked away until the end, and the rule for "who wins" was written down before the first run.

02 · What we found

Six findings, from the headline down.

Each finding has the short version first. Open "Show the numbers" for the evidence, or read the technical report for the full method.

FINDING 1

Where automation happens, the small model tied the frontier model.

Set to auto-accept about 70% of messages, Jev was right 91.4% of the time and Sonnet 5 91.0%. That difference is within noise: call it a tie. Haiku 4.5 trailed at 84.6%.

The chart shows every setting a team could choose, from "automate a little, very safely" on the left to "automate almost everything" on the right.

FINDING 2

It did it at one-fiftieth of the cost.

Jev cost $0.10 per 1,000 decisions. Sonnet 5 cost $5.25, 52 times more. Even the smaller Claude model, Haiku, cost 19 times more than Jev, while being less accurate.

FINDING 3

Cheap first, expensive only when needed, beat the expensive model alone.

Let Jev handle the messages it's sure about and pass the rest to Sonnet 5. That cascade answered every message with 82.8% accuracy for $1.51 per 1,000. Sonnet on its own: 82.3% for $5.25.

The best system wasn't a single model. It was a routing design.

FINDING 4

Jev's confidence can be dialed in. Claude's comes in round numbers.

Asked how sure they are, the Claude models mostly answer 85, 90 or 95. So many answers share the same score that a team can't set the automation level precisely. Jev's native scores are finer-grained.

At the "70%" setting, Jev actually auto-accepted 73% of messages, Sonnet 75%, and Haiku 81%.

Every automation level each model can be set to
Each dot is one achievable setting on the test set. More dots means finer control.
Show the numbers: calibration

Calibration asks: when a model says "90% sure", is it right 90% of the time? The gray line is perfect calibration. Sonnet 5 is the most trustworthy when it's confident. It just gives fewer, coarser scores.

FINDING 5

Plain rule-based checks caught mistakes for free.

Before trusting any confidence score, the pipeline runs simple Python checks. A keyword check ("does the message mention anything related to the chosen category?") stopped about 1 in 7 wrong answers while blocking under 2% of right ones.

And no invented category was ever auto-accepted. Jev can't produce one by design. Haiku produced two malformed replies, and the gate stopped both.

FINDING 6

Better instructions helped every model a little, and equally.

We rewrote the descriptions of the most-confused categories. That added about 1 point for Jev, and the same for Sonnet, so the rewrites didn't favor Jev.

A second round of rewrites gained nothing. And a 3.6-point lead Jev showed during tuning vanished on the locked test set, which is exactly why we held it back.

03 · Why it matters

What this means for teams building with AI.

Pick models by cost per trusted decision.

Leaderboards rank overall accuracy. In production you only automate the confident part, and there a small specialized model can match a frontier one. Sonnet was slightly better on all answers (82.3% vs 81.6%), which would have picked the wrong model.

Confidence is a product feature.

Your automation rate and your error rate both come from the confidence score. A model whose confidence can't be tuned finely is a model whose error rate you can't control precisely.

System design matters as much as the model.

Rule-based checks, a confidence bar, and a cascade to a stronger model did as much as the model choice. Because the pipeline stayed fixed, swapping models was a one-line change.

Discipline is what makes a result believable.

Locking the test set, writing the rule before the data, testing our own weak spot, and publishing every log. The tuning-set lead that disappeared shows why: the undisciplined version would have been wrong.

04 · Limits

The honest fine print.

  • One kind of task. Picking one of 77 fixed labels for short bank chat messages. Not email, open-ended writing, or reasoning.
  • One run per model. 1,000 test messages each. Differences under 1–2 points are noise.
  • Jev is a beta product. It launched in September 2026, and its pricing and versions may change. We pinned the version.
  • Claude's confidence was self-reported from a single call. Other ways of measuring its confidence might narrow Finding 4.
  • Descriptions weren't tuned on Claude's own mistakes. Sonnet at its very best could be somewhat higher.
  • Real emails come next. Subjects, signatures and threads make the job harder. That's phase 2.
